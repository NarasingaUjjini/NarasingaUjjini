How Does an Electric Guitar Really Work?

Every musician, engineer, or even casual listener has at some point encountered the electric guitar. Whether through rock music, jazz, or modern pop, its sound is unmistakable. But how exactly does an electric guitar produce sound? We shall explore the journey of a guitar signal, beginning at the vibrating string, moving through pickups, and finally reaching the amplifier and speaker.

The electric guitar begins not as an electrical system, but as a mechanical one. When a guitarist plucks a string, the string vibrates at a specific frequency. This frequency depends on the string’s length, tension, and mass. However, the vibration is not limited to a single frequency. Instead, it consists of a fundamental frequency along with higher-frequency components known as harmonics. These harmonics give the guitar its characteristic tone.

On its own, this vibrating string produces very little sound. Unlike an acoustic guitar, there is no hollow body to amplify the vibration. Therefore, the system requires a method to convert this mechanical vibration into something more useful: an electrical signal.

This conversion is performed by the pickup. A pickup consists of a magnet wrapped in a coil of wire. The guitar strings, typically made of steel, sit within the magnetic field produced by the magnet. When the string vibrates, it disturbs this magnetic field. According to electromagnetic principles, a changing magnetic field induces a voltage in the coil. Thus, the motion of the string is converted into a small, time-varying electrical signal.

At this stage, the signal is quite weak. It is an analog voltage, typically in the millivolt range, but it contains all the information about the string’s vibration—its frequency, harmonics, and dynamics. However, this signal is not yet strong enough to drive a speaker. It must first be processed and amplified.

This is the role of the amplifier, which can be thought of as consisting of two main stages: the preamplifier and the power amplifier.

The preamplifier serves to shape the signal. It increases the signal’s amplitude and allows for tonal adjustments through controls such as bass, mid, and treble. More importantly, it can introduce distortion when driven beyond its linear operating range. When this happens, the signal is no longer a perfect representation of the input. Instead, it becomes “clipped,” meaning its peaks are flattened. This process introduces additional harmonics into the signal, fundamentally altering its sound.

Following the preamp is the power amplifier, whose purpose is not to shape the signal, but to provide sufficient power to drive a speaker. While the preamp deals primarily with voltage, the power amp increases both voltage and current, allowing the system to deliver actual energy to the output device.

Finally, the signal reaches the speaker, which converts the electrical signal back into mechanical motion. A coil inside the speaker interacts with a magnetic field, causing a cone to move back and forth. This motion pushes air, creating sound waves that we can hear. Thus, the system completes its transformation: mechanical vibration to electrical signal, and back to mechanical motion in the form of sound.
