How does an electric guitar really work?

Beneath the wood and lacquer lies a miniature power plant relying on Faraday's law of induction to turn string vibration into high-voltage tone.

Byline:
Narasinga Ujjini

When a guitarist strikes a chord, the immediate visceral impact feels acoustic, physical. But the reality of a solid-body electric guitar is profoundly electromagnetic. It is, at its core, a transducer—a device designed specifically to convert kinetic energy into a tiny, alternating electrical current.

To understand this mechanism, one must peer into the heart of the instrument: the pickup. While it may look like a simple plastic bar studded with metal poles, it is actually a precisely wound induction coil sitting atop a permanent magnet.

The strings of an electric guitar are manufactured from ferromagnetic alloys, typically nickel-plated steel. When resting, these strings sit within the stationary magnetic field generated by the pickup's magnets. This field essentially magnetizes a small segment of the string directly above it.

" It is not the acoustic resonance of the wood that defines the primary electrical signal, but the disruption of the flux field by a vibrating mass of steel.

The magic happens upon plucking. As the magnetized string vibrates, it physically moves the magnetic field along with it. This moving magnetic field cuts across the thousands of turns of microscopic copper wire wound around the pickup bobbin. According to Faraday's law of induction, this fluctuating magnetic flux induces a corresponding alternating voltage in the coil.

This signal, though weak—usually measuring between 100 to 500 millivolts—contains the complex harmonic footprint of the string's vibration. It travels through the volume and tone potentiometers, out the output jack, and down an instrument cable to an amplifier, where silicon or vacuum tubes multiply its power until it can drive a speaker cone.
